Lab 11: AD Credential Attacks

Last week you walked out with a list of usernames and no passwords. This week you get the passwords, and you get them from Active Directory's own authentication system. Kerberos hands out tickets that are encrypted with account passwords, and it'll hand them to anyone who asks. You take them home and crack them offline, where no lockout and no alarm can stop you.

0. Where We Are

🧭
From a name to a password

Lab 10 gave you the map and a list of accounts. The obvious next question: how do you log in as one of them? Not by guessing passwords against the login (that locks accounts and sets off alarms). You abuse Kerberos to get a password hash you can crack on your own machine, quietly. This is the step that turns "I can see the domain" into "I am a user in the domain."

1. Why Kerberos Hands You Crackable Passwords · foundation

🎟️
The one design detail that powers these attacks

Kerberos avoids sending passwords over the network by using tickets. To prove who you are, the DC gives you a ticket, and some of those tickets are encrypted with the target account's password hash. That's fine as long as nobody can request a ticket they shouldn't. The problem: for certain accounts, anyone can. You ask the DC for the ticket, it encrypts it with the account's password, and you walk away with an offline guessing game you can run for as long as you like.

This is the number-one way real attackers get their first set of domain credentials, and it leaves almost no trace, because requesting a ticket is a normal, everyday thing.

Two accounts are vulnerable to two versions of the same trick. The difference is one setting, and it decides whether you even need a login first.

AS-REP Roasting

no login needed
  • 🎯 Targets accounts with "Kerberos pre-authentication" turned off. For those, the DC will send you an encrypted blob before you've proven anything. No credentials required at all.

Kerberoasting

any one account
  • 🔧 Targets service accounts (ones with an "SPN"). Any logged-in domain user can request their service ticket, encrypted with that service account's password. One cracked low-priv account gives you a shot at every service account.
🤔 You get an encrypted ticket but you can't read it without the account's password. Why is cracking it offline so much more dangerous than just trying passwords against the login page? Guess.
Answer

No lockouts and no logs. Guessing at the login fails after a few tries (account locked, alarms firing). But the encrypted ticket is already on your machine, so you run billions of guesses against it with hashcat, offline, for days if you want. The domain never sees a single attempt. That's why service accounts with weak passwords are a death sentence.

2. AS-REP Roasting · credential access

Same room as last week, the Attacktive Directory Domain Controller. Start with the easiest win: an account that doesn't require pre-authentication, so you can grab its hash with no credentials at all, using the usernames you enumerated in Lab 10.

🟢 Baseline · roast an account with no password

  1. Ask the DC for AS-REP hashes for your usernames. Accounts with pre-auth disabled give one up: 
    impacket-GetNPUsers <domain>/ -usersfile users.txt -no-pass -dc-ip <DC_IP>
  2. You get back a hash that starts $krb5asrep$23$. That's the account's password, encrypted. Save it to a file.
  3. Crack it offline with hashcat (mode 18200 is AS-REP): 
    hashcat -m 18200 hash.txt /usr/share/wordlists/rockyou.txt
  4. hashcat prints the cracked password next to the hash. You now have a real domain login.
the hash you're cracking · format, not a specific answer
$krb5asrep$23$<user>@<DOMAIN>:a1b2c3…(long encrypted blob)…

# hashcat -m 18200 chews through rockyou against this blob offline.
# when a guess decrypts it cleanly, that guess is the password.
# the actual account and password are what you recover on the box.

3. Kerberoasting · credential access

AS-REP only works on accounts with pre-auth off, which is rare. Kerberoasting is the one you'll use constantly, because it works against service accounts and only needs one valid login, the one you just cracked.

🟡 Stretch · roast the service accounts

  1. With any domain credentials, request service tickets for every service account: 
    impacket-GetUserSPNs <domain>/<user>:<pass> -dc-ip <DC_IP> -request
  2. Each ticket comes back as a $krb5tgs$23$ hash, encrypted with that service account's password.
  3. Crack them offline with hashcat (mode 13100 is Kerberoast): 
    hashcat -m 13100 spns.txt /usr/share/wordlists/rockyou.txt
⚖️
The difference, in one line

AS-REP needs no login but only works on accounts with pre-auth off (rare). Kerberoasting needs one login but works on every service account (common). Service accounts are the prize, because they often run with high privileges and have passwords set years ago and never changed.

4. Going Pro: Password Spraying Without Locking the Domain · credential access

Sometimes you crack one weak password and realize people reuse it. Spraying tries one password against every user, which avoids lockouts, but only if you do the math first. Getting this wrong locks out a whole company and ends your engagement.

🔴 Going Pro · read the policy, then spray

  1. First, read the lockout policy. You need the threshold (how many bad tries before a lock) and the window (how long it watches): 
    net accounts        # or query it over SMB with your cracked creds
  2. One password per user per window stays well under the threshold. Spray a likely password across all users at once: 
    netexec smb <DC_IP> -u users.txt -p 'Season2026!' --continue-on-success
  3. Read the hits. A reused password on a privileged account is often the shortest path to Domain Admin.

The lockout math is what separates a professional from someone who takes down the client's whole workforce. Always check the policy before you spray a single password.

5. Write the Finding · document

Kerberoastable service accounts with weak passwords are one of the most common high-impact findings in any AD test. The fix is unglamorous and almost never done: long, random, rotated service passwords.

📓 FINDING · Weak service account passwords recoverable via Kerberoasting severity: High
TitleService and pre-auth-disabled accounts expose crackable password hashes via Kerberos
SeverityHigh · CVSS 8.1 (any domain user recovers service account passwords, often privileged)
Affected accountsAccounts with an SPN (Kerberoastable) and any account with pre-auth disabled (AS-REP roastable)
Steps to reproduce1. impacket-GetNPUsers (no creds) or impacket-GetUserSPNs -request (one login) returns $krb5asrep$ / $krb5tgs$ hashes. 2. hashcat -m 18200 / -m 13100 against a wordlist recovers the plaintext. Evidence: the cracked password.
ImpactAn attacker with no (AS-REP) or minimal (Kerberoast) access recovers account passwords offline, undetected. Privileged service accounts lead straight toward Domain Admin.
RemediationGive service accounts long random passwords (25+ chars) or use group Managed Service Accounts (gMSA) that rotate automatically. Enable Kerberos pre-authentication for every account. Monitor for abnormal ticket requests.
