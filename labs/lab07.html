1. You Can't Hack What You Can't See · foundation

📡
October 2016: a botnet built entirely by scanning

The Mirai botnet did one thing: it fired probes at random IP addresses across the whole internet, looking for one open port, TCP 23 (Telnet), left on by default on cheap cameras and routers. Every device that answered got a handful of factory passwords tried against it. Hundreds of thousands of devices answered. On October 21st, 2016, Mirai pointed all of them at the DNS provider Dyn and knocked Twitter, Reddit, Netflix, and Spotify off the internet for most of a day.

No clever exploit. The entire attack was recon: find the open port, try the obvious door. Attackers scan the whole internet constantly. Your job today is to scan one target properly, which is harder than it sounds.

Enumeration is the part beginners rush and pros obsess over. The exploit is usually easy once you've found the right service; finding it is the work. The rule the whole week runs on: a port is just a number until you know exactly what's listening on it and which version.

🤔 You scan a box and find port 80 open. A teammate says "cool, it's a website, let's go." What's the one thing you still don't know that decides whether this is a 5-minute win or a dead end? Write your guess.
Answer

What's actually running there, and which version. Port 80 could be a patched, boring Nginx, or an ancient Apache with a public exploit, or a login panel for admin software with default creds. "Port 80 is open" is almost no information. "Port 80 runs Apache 2.4.49" is a lead you can look up. That gap is the difference between the two tiers of this lab.

2. How a Scan Actually Works · foundation

Remember the TCP handshake from Lab 01: SYN → SYN/ACK → ACK. A port scan is just that handshake, started on purpose against every port, watching who answers. Nmap has two main ways to do it, and the difference matters on a real engagement.

-sT · TCP connect

no root needed 
SYN  →
     ← SYN/ACK
ACK  →          full handshake,
                then hang up
  • 📝 Completes the connection, so the target's app logs it. Reliable, but loud.

-sS · SYN / half-open

needs sudo 
SYN  →
     ← SYN/ACK
RST  →          tear it down before
                the connection completes
  • 🥷 Never finishes the handshake, so many apps never log it. Faster, quieter, the default when you have root.
🔎
Open vs closed, in one line

A SYN/ACK back means the port is open (something's listening). A RST back means closed (nobody home). Silence usually means a firewall dropped your packet. Same three answers you learned in Lab 01, now you're reading them off a scan.

🟢 Baseline · the scan you'll run on every box forever

  1. Run the bread-and-butter scan against the target IP: 
    nmap -sC -sV -oN scan.txt <TARGET_IP>
  2. Read every line. Don't skim. Each open port with its service and version is a lead.
  3. Save it (-oN scan.txt) so you're not re-scanning every two minutes. Your scan output is your notes.
nmap -sC -sV · representative output from a vulnerable box
PORT     STATE SERVICE     VERSION
22/tcp   open  ssh         OpenSSH 7.2p2 Ubuntu 4ubuntu2.8
80/tcp   open  http        Apache httpd 2.4.18 ((Ubuntu))
139/tcp  open  netbios-ssn Samba smbd 3.X - 4.X
445/tcp  open  netbios-ssn Samba smbd 4.3.11-Ubuntu

# this is the shape of nmap output, not a specific room's answer.

🤔 Look at the output above. SSH on 22, a web server on 80, Samba on 139/445, Tomcat on 8080. If you only get to look at one service first, which do you pick, and why? Guess.
Answer

There's no single right answer, and that's the lesson. SSH is almost never the way in (you'd need creds). The web ports (80, 8080) and Samba (139/445) are where things usually live. A pentester reads versions first and pivots to whichever one looks oldest or most exposed. The skill is not having a favorite port, it's reading the whole map before you move.

🔴 Going Pro · the enumeration loop a real tester runs

  1. Scan everything. nmap -sC -sV -oN scan.txt <IP>. Note every open port.
  2. Enumerate each service in turn. Web → gobuster. SMB → enum4linux -a. Read the version of each and look it up.
  3. Collect the loose threads. A hidden directory, a username off SMB, a readable share, an old service version. Write each one down.
  4. Pick the entry point. The way in is usually the oldest service, the exposed share, or the hidden page, not the hardened SSH. Pull the thread that looks weakest.

This loop is the entire job in Weeks 7 and 8. Boxes don't get hacked by one magic command, they get hacked by someone who enumerated patiently and noticed the one thing out of place.
