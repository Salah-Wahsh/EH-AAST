Lab 13: Reporting & the Engagement Lifecycle

Here's the part nobody posts about: the client never watches you hack. They read your report. It's the only thing they pay for and the only thing they keep. Today you turn the findings you've written all semester into a professional report.

0. Where We Are

🧭
The step every lab has been feeding

Recon → Map → Identify → Exploit → Document. You've written one finding at the end of every lab all semester, on purpose. This week you learn to score them properly, write them so a stranger can reproduce them, and tie them into one story an executive will actually act on. 1. The Report Is the Product · foundation

📄
You can hack everything and still fail

The OSCP exam, the entry-level cert this course aims at, works like a real engagement: you compromise a set of machines, then you have a full day to write the report. People who root every box and write a sloppy report fail. People who root fewer boxes but document them cleanly pass. The industry built it that way on purpose, because a finding nobody can understand or reproduce is worth nothing to the client who has to fix it.

Your job isn't to prove you're clever. It's to hand the client a clear, prioritized, reproducible list of what's broken and how to fix it.

A good report has two audiences in one document. The executive summary is for the people who sign the cheques: short, plain, business risk. The technical findings are for the engineers who fix it: exact, reproducible, with the command that proves it. Write for both, or you lose half your readers.

🤔 A junior writes: "Found SQL injection on the login page. Recommend sanitizing input." What are the two biggest things wrong with that as a professional finding? Guess.
Answer

First, it's not reproducible: no exact URL, parameter, payload, or evidence, so the developer can't confirm or test the fix. Second, the remediation is a buzzword: "sanitize input" is what people say when they don't know the real fix, which here is parameterized queries. A good finding lets someone reproduce it in two minutes and fix it correctly on the first try.

2. Anatomy of a Finding · foundation

You've been writing these all semester in the "Write the Finding" section of every lab. Here's the full skeleton, and why each part exists.

Title What and where, in one line. "SQL injection in the login form."
Severity + CVSS A number and a rating so the client can prioritize. From §3.
Affected endpoint/asset The exact URL, parameter, host, or account.
Steps to reproduce The exact payload/command, numbered, so anyone can repeat it.
Impact What an attacker gets in business terms, not just "RCE."
Remediation The specific, correct fix. Not "sanitize input."

3. Score It with CVSS v3.1 · document

"High" and "Critical" aren't opinions, they come from a formula. CVSS turns a bug into a 0–10 number from eight questions about how it's exploited and what it costs. You don't memorize the math, you use the calculator and understand the inputs.

The eight metrics (what each asks)
AV · Attack VectorNetwork, adjacent, local, or physical? Remote is worse.
AC · Attack ComplexityDoes it just work, or need special conditions?
PR · Privileges RequiredNone, low, or high? No login needed is worse.
UI · User InteractionDoes a victim have to click something?
S · ScopeDoes it break out beyond the vulnerable component?
C / I / AConfidentiality, Integrity, Availability impact: none/low/high.

🎯 Open the official CVSS v3.1 calculator
🤔 Both bugs are "remote, no login, easy." Yet SQLi scores 9.8 and reflected XSS only 6.1. Which single metric does most of that gap? Guess.
Answer

User Interaction (plus the impact levels). Reflected XSS needs a victim to click your link (UI:R), and it only leaks a bit at a time (C:L/I:L). SQLi fires on its own (UI:N) and reads the whole database (C:H/I:H). "Needs a click" and "full database" are what separate a 9.8 from a 6.1. That's why you can't eyeball severity, the metrics decide it.

4. Bad Finding vs Good Finding · document

Same bug, two write-ups. One gets ignored, one gets fixed. The difference is everything you'll be judged on.

💥 The junior version

gets ignored
  • ❌ "Found SQLi on login. Fix: sanitize input."
  • ❌ No URL, no payload, no evidence.
  • ❌ No severity, no impact.
  • ❌ A fix that's a buzzword, not a fix.

✅ The professional version

gets fixed
  • ✔️ Title, CVSS 9.8, affected endpoint POST /login.
  • ✔️ Exact payload ' OR 1=1-- and the response proving bypass.
  • ✔️ Impact: full auth bypass + database read.
  • ✔️ Fix: parameterized queries, with a code example.

5. Going Pro: The Executive Summary and Attack Chain · document

Individual findings are the technical half. The executives read one page: the summary. The most powerful thing you can put there is the attack chain, the story of how small issues combined into total compromise. You lived that chain this semester.

🔴 Going Pro · tell the chain in one paragraph

YOUR SEMESTER, AS ONE ATTACK CHAIN

Write that as three or four plain sentences a CFO understands: "An attacker with no access uploaded a file to the website, used it to run commands on the server, escalated to full control of that machine, and from there took over the company's entire Windows domain. Each step used a common, fixable misconfiguration." No jargon. Just the line from "anyone on the internet" to "game over," and the fact that every link was preventable.
